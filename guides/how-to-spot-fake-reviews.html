Reviews are the closest thing buyers have to word of mouth at scale, which is exactly why they attract manipulation. Regulators have noticed: in the United States the Federal Trade Commission's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, in force since October 2024, bans fake reviews, buying reviews conditioned on a positive or negative sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews and review suppression. Rules alone do not clean up every page, though, so it pays to read reviews like an investigator.

Look at patterns, not single reviews

One suspicious review proves little. Manipulation shows up as a pattern across many reviews, so zoom out before you zoom in.

  1. Bursts of reviews in a short window. A product with three reviews a month that suddenly receives forty in a week, mostly five stars, deserves scrutiny. Genuine volume tends to track sales and seasonality.
  2. A rating distribution with no middle. Real products collect some three- and four-star reviews. A profile that is almost entirely five stars plus a handful of one-star complaints often signals purchased praise drowning out real experience.
  3. Repeated phrases. When several reviewers use the same unusual phrase, product nickname or sentence structure, they may be working from the same brief.
  4. Vague superlatives with no specifics. "Best company ever, highly recommend!" tells you nothing. Genuine reviewers mention what they bought, what happened and what they would change.

Check the reviewer, not just the review

  1. Thin or brand-new profiles. Accounts created the same day as the review, with one review ever, are weaker evidence than reviewers with a history across different businesses.
  2. Reviewers who only ever give five stars — or only ever give one star — to businesses in the same niche.
  3. Geography that does not fit. A local plumber reviewed mostly by accounts in other countries is a warning sign.
  4. Undisclosed relationships. Employees, owners' relatives and affiliates must disclose their connection. If a review reads like marketing copy and mentions internal details, assume a relationship until shown otherwise.

Read the content critically

  1. Competitor name-dropping. Reviews that praise one vendor while attacking a named competitor are a classic tactic, in both directions.
  2. AI-generated polish. Generated reviews tend to be balanced in a generic way, list features the way a product page does and avoid concrete moments. Ask yourself: could this have been written without using the product?
  3. Mismatch with the product. Reviews that describe features the product does not have, or a different version, may have been moved from another listing.
  4. Owner responses that only appear on positive reviews. Healthy businesses respond to criticism. Silence on every negative review — or aggressive, legalistic replies — tells you how complaints are handled.

Read the three-star reviews first

The most informative reviews are usually in the middle. Three-star reviewers liked enough to keep using the product and are specific about what fell short. Sort by "most recent" too: a business that changed owners or pricing last quarter may be very different from the one praised two years ago.

Cross-check across sources

No single platform is immune. Compare a vendor's reviews across several independent sites, ask the vendor for two references in your industry and, for B2B purchases, request a trial with your own data. A vendor confident in its product will rarely refuse.
