Most happy customers never leave a review simply because nobody asked at the right moment. The fix is not a trick; it is a system. This guide covers how to ask, when to ask and what you are not allowed to do.

First, the rules

Review platforms and regulators draw the same lines:

  • Do not buy reviews or offer rewards conditioned on a positive review. The U.S. FTC rule on consumer reviews explicitly bans incentives tied to sentiment. Many platforms, including Google, prohibit incentives for reviews of any kind.
  • Do not "gate" reviews by asking only satisfied customers to post publicly while routing unhappy ones to a private form. Several platforms treat this as manipulation.
  • Do not write reviews of your own business or ask employees and family to do so without clear disclosure.
  • Do not suppress negative reviews through threats, non-disparagement clauses or false reports to platforms.

Everything below stays within those limits.

Ask at the moment of value

The best time to ask is right after the customer experiences the result, not right after they pay. For a restaurant that is the end of the meal; for a SaaS product, after the first successful project or report; for a contractor, at the final walkthrough.

Remove every bit of friction

  • Send a direct link to the review form, not to your profile page.
  • Keep the request short: who you are, why it matters, one link.
  • Make it mobile-friendly; most reviews are written on phones.
  • Send one reminder after a few days, then stop.

Make asking a habit, not a campaign

A steady flow of reviews beats a one-off blitz. Recency matters to buyers, and sudden bursts can trigger platform filters. Build the request into your workflow: the invoice template, the job-completion checklist, the onboarding sequence.

Respond to every review

Responding shows future customers that someone is listening, and customers who see responses are more likely to leave a review themselves. Thank positive reviewers specifically, and handle criticism calmly — see our guide to responding to negative reviews.

Measure what matters

  • Request-to-review rate: reviews received ÷ requests sent. Typical rates vary widely, so track your own trend.
  • Review velocity: new reviews per month.
  • Average rating trend over the last 90 days versus all time.
